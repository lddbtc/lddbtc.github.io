What a Real China Inspection Report Should Contain

Buyers often receive a PDF labelled 'inspection report' that proves nothing: a few stock photos, a green PASS stamp and no data. A genuine third-party report is an evidence document. Here is the checklist to judge one — whether it comes from a large inspection company or an independent inspector.

The 12 elements of a usable report

1. Inspector name and the date and time on site. 2. Client, supplier and factory names, plus the exact factory address visited. 3. Purchase order or reference number. 4. Product description, specification and approved sample reference. 5. Order quantity versus quantity presented for inspection. 6. The sampling standard used (commonly ISO 2859-1 / ANSI Z1.4) with the sample size drawn. 7. The agreed AQL levels for critical, major and minor defects. 8. A defect table with counts for each classification. 9. Measurements and test results against the spec, including the instruments used. 10. Packaging, labelling, barcode and carton marking checks. 11. Original timestamped photos of the actual goods, the cartons, the workshop and any defects. 12. A clear verdict — pass, conditional or fail — with the reasons and the inspector's signature.

If a document is missing most of items 5–10, it is a summary, not an inspection report. It cannot support a payment decision or a claim.

How to read the AQL table

AQL sampling means a defined number of units are drawn at random from the presented lot; each unit is classified as acceptable or defective, and defects are graded critical (unsafe or unusable), major (likely to cause a customer return) and minor (cosmetic). The report should show the acceptance and rejection numbers for each class, and the actual count found.

A 'pass' with zero detail is meaningless. Ask to see the sample size, the numbers found in each class, and the photographs of the defects that were found but still fell within the limit. That is the difference between a real check and a formality.

Red flags that a report is copied or fake

Photos that look like catalogue images, have no timestamps, or show a different product variant. Identical defect counts across several orders. No factory address or no inspector name. A report issued before production was finished. A supplier who sends 'their own QC report' instead of allowing an independent visit. And any report where the verdict cannot be traced back to a number in a table.

Ask the inspector one specific question about the production line or the packaging that day. The person who was actually on the floor will be able to answer it.

Use the report as a commercial tool

Attach the report to your balance-payment approval, keep it with the container loading record, and file it against the purchase order. If defects are found, the report is the basis for requesting rework and a re-inspection rather than an argument about opinions.
