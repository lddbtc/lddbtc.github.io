Pre-shipment inspection checklist for importers

A pre-shipment inspection (PSI) only protects you when it is done on the finished lot, after production, by someone who is not paid by the factory. Here is what a real one covers.

Home › PSI checklist

When to book the inspection

Book it for when 100% of the order is produced and at least 80% packed. Inspect too early and you miss the bulk; inspect at the port and the goods are already containerised. Tell me the product, order quantity and expected ready date and I schedule the on-site day.

AQL sampling — the standard buyers use

Most importers inspect to AQL (Acceptance Quality Limit) Level II, the default in ISO 2859-1. The sample size and accept/reject numbers come from the AQL table based on your order quantity. Common plans:

Critical defects

Safety, regulatory, labelling law. Acceptable number is almost always zero.

Major defects

AQL 2.5 typical — affects function or salability. A few are tolerable, many are not.

Minor defects

AQL 4.0 typical — cosmetic only. More lenient, but tracked.

The on-site checklist

  • Quantity — cartons counted, pieces per carton verified against the packing list.
  • Workmanship — visual defects, assembly, finish, against your approved sample.
  • Function — live testing of key features, not just a power-on.
  • Appearance & colour — against the sealed golden sample you kept.
  • Labels & markings — carton marks, care labels, barcodes, country-of-origin, compliance marks (CE, UL, etc.).
  • Packaging — inner/outer carton, drop test if agreed, suitability for long sea freight.
  • Assortment — sizes/colours ratio matches the order; no silent substitutions.

What a real report contains

Original, timestamped photos and video taken by the inspector on site — never stock imagery. A straight English verdict with the AQL result, defect photos, and a clear "ship / hold / rework" recommendation. Independence is what makes the verdict trustworthy.

Common traps a checklist catches

  • Mixed SKUs — wrong colour or model slipped into the carton. Starts at the loading bay.
  • Short shipment — fewer pieces than invoiced; only a count catches it.
  • Old-stock fill — near-expiry or previously rejected units repacked as new.
  • Fake compliance marks — a CE or UL mark the product was never certified for.
